ANATOMY OF THE RETEST IN THE QQQ
    OPENING RANGE BREAKOUT
    Exploratory Evidence on Price Behavior Following the
               QQQ Opening Range Breakout


                                       Mario Pineda

ABSTRACT
This paper is an exploratory and descriptive study. The author makes no claims of causality or
exploitability regarding the documented patterns.
We present an exploratory study of the post-breakout sequence in the Invesco QQQ Trust ETF, using
intraday data at one-minute resolution from January 2017 through December 2024. The existing
literature on the Opening Range Breakout focuses on entry at the moment of the breakout; the internal
dynamics of the minutes that follow have not been measured to date.
We decompose the sequence into four levels — the opening range, the breakout, the retest, and the
outcome — and document the statistical associations observed at each level. We find that range size
is associated with the magnitude of the subsequent move, that a consistent fraction of breakouts
generates a retest of the broken level within a defined time window, that the time elapsed until that
retest shows the strongest association with the subsequent outcome, and that larger pre-retest
excursions are associated with a lower probability of continuation — a counterintuitive pattern that
we document along with its interpretive limitations.
All results are descriptive associations conditional on operational definitions that we document with
precision. Causal mechanisms are unknown. We invite other researchers to replicate, challenge, and
extend this work.




Keywords: Opening Range Breakout, intraday momentum, breakout retest, QQQ, ETF microstructure
1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 The Starting Point
The Opening Range Breakout is one of the most studied and longest-standing intraday trading
strategies in financial markets. Its premise is simple: the price range established during the first
minutes of the trading session reflects the accumulated order imbalance from the previous night, and
a decisive move beyond that range signals sufficient directional conviction for the market to continue
in that direction for the remainder of the day.
Despite its long history, the strategy retains both academic and practical relevance. The most recent
and rigorous work — Zarattini and Aziz (2023) and Zarattini, Barbon, and Aziz (2024), distributed
through the Swiss Finance Institute — documented that a five-minute ORB strategy applied to QQQ
from 2016 through 2023 produced an annualized alpha of 33% net of commissions. These results
place the ORB among the few market anomalies supported by solid academic evidence.
However, all prior literature shares one common feature: the position is initiated at the moment of the
breakout and managed from that point forward. What occurs in the minutes following the breakout
— whether price retraces to the level it just broke, whether that retrace has measurable properties,
and whether those properties are associated with subsequent price behavior — has never been
systematically measured. That is the gap this paper begins to address.
1.2 The Questions Guiding This Paper
This paper does not aim to demonstrate that the retest is exploitable in trading, nor that the patterns
we find will persist in the future. The objective is more modest and, we believe, more honest: to
measure for the first time what occurs after the ORB breakout in QQQ, with sufficient methodological
rigor for other researchers to replicate, challenge, or extend the findings.
The four questions guiding the analysis are as follows. What fraction of ORB breakouts generates a
retest of the broken level, under the conditions we define? What are the properties of the price
excursion prior to that retest? How long does the retest take to occur? Are the conditions under which
the retest occurs associated with subsequent price behavior, as classified by our definitions?
The explicit inclusion of the phrases "under the conditions we define" and "as classified by our
definitions" in those questions is not accidental. It reflects the approach we adopt throughout this
paper: being precise about what each number we report measures, and what it does not.
1.3 Why QQQ and Why This Period
QQQ was selected for three concrete reasons. First, it is one of the most liquid instruments in the
global equity market, with bid-ask spreads at one-minute resolution typically ranging from $0.01 to
$0.02, allowing price dynamics to be studied without the microstructure noise that contaminates less
liquid assets. Second, it tracks the Nasdaq-100, with massive participation from both institutional and
individual investors, ensuring that the observed patterns reflect genuine market behavior with
sufficient depth. Third, the most relevant prior literature uses QQQ as its primary instrument, enabling
direct comparisons with existing work.
The 2017–2024 period spans five qualitatively distinct market regimes: a low-volatility bull market
(2017–2019), the COVID-19 crisis and subsequent recovery (2020), an accelerated bull market
(2021), a sustained bear market driven by the Federal Reserve's monetary tightening with a 32.6%
decline (2022), and a recovery to new highs (2023–2024). This diversity of conditions is important
for evaluating whether the observed patterns are specific to a particular type of market or appear with
broader generality.
1.4 Scope of the Study
This paper is a descriptive and exploratory study of a single instrument over a specific period. It
neither implements nor evaluates trading strategies. It does not incorporate transaction costs or
execution slippage. It makes no claim that the patterns found are causal or that they will persist outside
the sample studied.
A caveat the reader should keep in mind from the outset: in a study such as this, operational definitions
are not neutral conventions. They are decisions that determine what is measured and, therefore, what
is found. In particular, the way we define the Continuation outcome and the Failure outcome can
mechanically influence some of the numerical results we report. We document these effects with
precision in Section 3 and revisit them each time they are relevant. This transparency about the
limitations of our own definitions is, in our view, an essential part of the contribution this paper seeks
to make.
1.5 Structure of the Paper
Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 describes the data, the analytical framework, and,
with particular care, the operational definitions of all measured events and their possible effects on
the results. Sections 4 through 7 present the results for each level of the analysis. Section 8 presents
the multivariate analysis. Section 9 presents the robustness analysis. Section 10 discusses the
observed patterns, their possible interpretations as unverified hypotheses, and their limitations.
Section 11 concludes with the questions this paper leaves open for future research.



2. RELATED LITERATURE
2.1 The ORB in Academia
Academic research on the ORB begins formally with Holmberg, Lönnbark, and Lundström (2012),
who proposed a statistical framework for evaluating the profitability of ORB strategies using daily
OHLC data. Applied to crude oil futures from 1983 to 2011, they found significantly positive average
returns concentrated in periods of elevated volatility. Lundström (2017) extended this finding,
documenting that ORB profitability grows monotonically with realized volatility. This result is
relevant as context for the patterns we observe in Level 1 regarding opening range size.
Tsai, Wang, and collaborators (2018) introduced the concept of the Timely Opening Range Breakout,
using one-minute data across five index futures markets from 2003 to 2013. Their main finding was
that the optimal range formation period is short — typically five minutes or less — and that the quality
of the breakout signal decays rapidly with the time of day. This is consistent with the patterns we
observe in Level 2.
The most directly relevant work is the series of papers by Zarattini and collaborators. Zarattini and
Aziz (2023) investigated ORB profitability in QQQ using a five-minute range, documenting an
annualized alpha of 33% and a Sharpe ratio above 2.0. Zarattini, Barbon, and Aziz (2024) extended
the analysis to more than 7,000 U.S. stocks.
None of these papers studies the post-breakout retest. Entry occurs on the breakout candle and
position management begins from that point. The internal dynamics of the minutes that follow are
not addressed in any prior work known to the authors.
2.2 Intraday Microstructure as Theoretical Context
Chordia, Roll, and Subrahmanyam (2002) documented that order imbalances at the start of the session
are substantially larger than at other times of day. This is consistent with the concentration of
breakouts at the open that we document in Level 2, although it does not establish causality between
the two phenomena.
Dim, Eraker, and Vilkov (2023) documented that market maker inventory gamma predicts intraday
price reversals. We mention this work as context for the interpretive hypotheses proposed in the
Discussion. We do not verify those hypotheses directly, and it should not be understood as evidence
that the mechanism operates in our data.
2.3 Positioning of This Paper
This paper differs from the existing literature in three concrete respects. First, it disaggregates the
post-breakout sequence into measurable components with statistical properties that can be studied
separately. Second, the retest is operationalized using a precise, pre-specified definition that allows
for reproducibility. Third, the questions are descriptive rather than prescriptive — we seek not the
optimal strategy but the properties of the phenomenon as it appears in the data under our definitions.



3. DATA AND METHODOLOGY
3.1 The Thinking Behind the Design
Before formally describing the data, it is worth explaining the logic that led to this design. The original
question was simple: after price breaks the QQQ opening range, what happens?
The most immediate answer is that price sometimes continues in the direction of the breakout and
sometimes reverses. But that is too vague to serve as a useful scientific starting point. What we want
to know is whether there is something observable — in the range that preceded the breakout, in the
breakout itself, or in the minutes that follow — that is associated with one outcome or the other.
To answer that in an orderly way, we designed a framework that moves from the general to the
specific. It makes no sense to jump directly to the outcome without first characterizing the context in
which that outcome occurs. That chain of reasoning led to the design of four sequential levels: first
the range, then the breakout, then the retest, then the outcome. Each level builds on the previous one,
and its results serve as input for the next.
3.2 The Data
The dataset consists of one-minute OHLCV bars — open, high, low, close, and volume — for the
Invesco QQQ Trust from January 26, 2017 through December 31, 2024, covering the regular trading
session from 9:30 AM to 3:59 PM Eastern Time. All timestamps are converted to Eastern Time with
explicit UTC offset handling to ensure correct treatment of daylight saving time transitions.
3.3 Why One-Minute Data
The choice of one-minute resolution reflects a balance between granularity and noise. Daily data does
not allow the internal sequence of the breakout to be studied. Tick or second-level data adds
microstructure noise that complicates the identification of meaningful events without providing
substantive additional information for the questions of this paper. One-minute resolution allows
precise measurement of when the breakout occurs, when the retest occurs, and how long each event
takes, while filtering out smaller-scale fluctuations.
3.4 The Four-Level Analytical Framework
The analysis is organized into four sequential levels, where each level builds on the outputs of the
previous one. This architecture reflects the natural temporal sequence of the phenomenon: first the
range forms, then the breakout occurs, then the retest may occur, and finally the subsequent price
behavior is observed. The goal of this structure is to identify at which point in the sequence the
information associated with the outcome is concentrated, and whether that information is statistically
independent across levels.
3.5 Opening Range Durations

All analyses are conducted in parallel for 24 durations from 5 to 120 minutes in 5-minute increments.
This decision reflects the absence of prior empirical consensus on the optimal range duration for QQQ
specifically. Results are reported for the 15-minute range as the representative case, selected because
it yields an adequate sample size and a formation period long enough to absorb the most volatile
minutes of the open. Where patterns differ materially across durations, this is documented explicitly.

3.6 Operational Definitions and Their Relationship to the Results

This is the most important section of the paper for any reader who wishes to interpret the results
correctly. In an empirical study such as this, operational definitions are not merely technical
conventions: they are decisions that determine what enters the sample, how each observation is
classified, and consequently what numbers appear in the tables. A result that appears to describe a
market phenomenon may in part reflect the properties of the definition used to measure it.

We present each definition alongside an explicit note on how it may affect the results. All definitions
were fixed prior to running any analysis, which is important for transparency, but does not eliminate
the effects described below.

Opening Range (OR): The price range defined by the highest high and lowest low of all one-minute
bars from 9:30 AM to 9:30 AM + N minutes, where N ∈ {5, 10, 15, ..., 120}.

Breakout: The first one-minute bar whose high exceeds the OR high (bullish breakout) or whose low
falls below the OR low (bearish breakout). Only the first breakout of the day is analyzed for each
range duration.
Note on the selection bias introduced by this definition: By analyzing only the first breakout of the
day, we exclude days with multiple breakouts and days with no breakout. The resulting sample does
not represent all QQQ trading days but a subset with specific properties. The patterns we document
apply to that subset, and their generalization to other contexts requires caution.

Valid Retest: A post-breakout event that satisfies three conditions sequentially and in order.

Condition 1 — Minimum 0.20% excursion: After the breakout, price must reach a minimum favorable
excursion of 0.20% of the breakout price before any pullback is considered a valid retest. This
threshold was chosen to separate genuine retests from bars that simply oscillate around the level
without price having established a defined direction. At QQQ prices between $130 and $500, 0.20%
represents between $0.26 and $1.00.

Condition 2 — Level contact, ±0.05% tolerance: After satisfying Condition 1, price must return to
the breakout level within a tolerance of 0.05% of the breakout price. This tolerance acknowledges
that price levels have finite width in practice and an exact price is rarely touched to the cent.

Condition 3 — 120-minute window: The retest must occur within 120 minutes of the breakout. Retests
occurring after that window are considered to belong to a different intraday context.

Note on the sensitivity of these conditions: The robustness analysis in Section 9 shows that the relative
gap between fast and slow retests ranges from 36.7 to 48.3 percentage points across all threshold
specifications explored. This suggests that the temporal pattern of the retest does not depend critically
on the exact values chosen, although the absolute figures change with the thresholds.

Continuation Outcome: Within 60 minutes after the retest, price exceeds the maximum favorable
pre-retest excursion — that is, it surpasses the furthest point it had reached between the breakout and
the retest. We refer to that point as MFE_pre.

Note on the mechanical effect of this definition: This is the most important definition to understand
in terms of its implications for the results. The bar that price must clear to be classified as a
Continuation is the MFE_pre. When the MFE_pre is small — for example, 0.25% — that bar is
relatively easy to clear. When the MFE_pre is large — for example, 0.80% — the bar is substantially
higher and harder to reach within the 60-minute window. This creates a mechanical relationship
between MFE_pre and the Continuation rate: the larger the MFE_pre, the more demanding the
definition, regardless of what the market does.

The fourth pattern we document in Level 4 — that larger MFE_pre is associated with a lower
Continuation rate — may be partially or entirely produced by this mechanical effect. We cannot
determine with certainty how much of that pattern reflects a genuine market phenomenon and how
much reflects the increasing difficulty imposed by the definition. We report the pattern because it is
statistically robust and because the question of what produces it is itself valuable, but the reader
should keep this caveat in mind when interpreting that result.

One way to disentangle the two effects in future research would be to redefine Continuation using a
fixed target — for example, an additional 0.20% move beyond the retest price — rather than the
variable target used here. If the MFE_pre pattern persists under that alternative definition, it would
constitute stronger evidence that it reflects a genuine market phenomenon.

Failure Outcome: Within 60 minutes after the retest, price crosses the opposite boundary of the
opening range — OR_low for bullish breakouts, OR_high for bearish breakouts — before achieving
a Continuation Outcome.

Note on the mechanical effect of this definition: The failure barrier is the opposite extreme of the OR.
When the opening range is wider, that opposite extreme is farther from the breakout price. Therefore,
the Failure definition requires a larger move on days with wide ranges than on days with narrow
ranges. This may explain, at least in part, the observed pattern whereby larger ranges are associated
with lower Failure rates — not necessarily because the phenomenon is different, but because the
definition places the barrier farther away. The reader should keep this in mind when interpreting the
results in Table 10 and Table 13.

Neutral Outcome: Neither of the above occurs within 60 minutes of the retest. The analysis in
Section 9 shows that these cases resolve approximately 50/50 between Continuation and Failure when
the window is extended to the session close, suggesting they represent genuine market indecision
within the original window.

3.7 Sample Flow Diagram

To facilitate exact replication of the analysis, we document the filtering process step by step for the
15-minute OR case, which is the representative case used throughout the paper.

The dataset begins with 2,290 trading days. Of these, 2,288 days yield a valid opening range with
sufficient bars for full formation. Of those 2,288 days, all produce at least one OR breakout during
the session, but only the first is analyzed. Of the 2,288 breakouts, 1,565 (68.4%) meet the minimum
0.20% excursion criterion in the direction of the breakout before retracing. Of those 1,565, a total of
1,045 (66.8%) generate a valid retest within the 120-minute window. Those 1,045 retests constitute
the final Level 4 sample. Of them, 482 (46.1%) produce a Continuation Outcome, 195 (18.7%)
produce a Failure Outcome, and 368 (35.2%) produce a Neutral Outcome within the 60-minute
window.
Differences in N across tables in the paper — 2,290, 2,288, 2,277, and other values — reflect this
sequential filtering process across different analyses and OR durations. Each table reports the N

4. LEVEL 1 — ANATOMY OF THE OPENING RANGE
4.1 The Question and Why We Start Here

The first level aims to characterize the opening range before any breakout occurs. The reason for
starting here is that the range is the context in which everything else takes place. If we want to
understand what is associated with price behavior after the retest, we first need to understand the
conditions under which that retest occurs. The opening range is the initial state of the system.

In the observed data, the average MFE — the maximum price excursion from the breakout level to
the session close — grows monotonically from 0.41% in the minimum range quintile to 1.15% in the
largest, a difference of 2.8 times. This association holds without exception across every quintile and

What this association describes, in concrete terms, is that days on which QQQ establishes a wider
opening range tend to produce breakouts with greater subsequent excursion, under the MFE definition
we use. Why this association occurs is a separate question that these data do not answer. One possible
hypothesis is that range size reflects the degree of imbalance between buyers and sellers during range
formation, and that this imbalance persists after the breakout. But this is an interpretive hypothesis,
not a result.

4.3 Pattern 1.2 — The Closing Position of the OR Within the Range Is Associated with the
Direction of the Subsequent Breakout

The last two columns of Table 2 show where the OR formation period closed within its own range,
separated by the direction of the breakout that subsequently occurred. The OR_close_position
variable takes a value of 0 when the close is exactly the range low and 1 when it is exactly the range
high.
What is observed is consistent across all quintiles: when the subsequent breakout was bullish, the OR
had closed on average in the upper 65–73% of the range. When the breakout was bearish, it had closed
in the lower 25–40%. This information is observable before the breakout. However, the distribution
of bullish versus bearish breakouts is approximately 50/50 across all quintiles, indicating that this
variable does not predict direction with sufficient certainty to constitute a directional signal on its
own.

the problem of direction.

observer does not only see the range that has formed — they also see how the breakout occurs: at

occur, or how the price excursion before it is distributed.

7. LEVEL 4 — THE OUTCOME
7.1 The Central Question and Its Accompanying Caveat

We now reach the level where the central question of the paper is answered: given that a valid retest
occurred under our definitions, what is observed in the following 60 minutes, as classified by our
outcome definitions?

This framing of the question — explicitly conditional on the definitions — is intentional. The numbers

8. MULTIVARIATE ANALYSIS
8.1 Purpose

Levels 1 through 4 analyzed each variable independently. This analysis has two distinct objectives.
The first is to verify whether the observed effects persist when mutually controlled — in particular,
whether the association between mins_to_retest and the outcome is independent of breakout time,
which is a potential source of confounding. The second is to measure the correlation among variables
in order to assess whether they capture genuinely distinct dimensions of the phenomenon.

8.2 Logistic Models

We estimate four logistic models with the dependent variable equal to 1 for Continuation outcomes
and 0 otherwise. All continuous variables were standardized — zero mean, unit standard deviation
— so that coefficients are comparable across variables. The simplification of treating Failure and
Neutral outcomes as a combined category is a methodological limitation: information about the
distinction between the two is lost, which may bias the estimated probabilities.

10. DISCUSSION
10.1 On the Time-to-Retest Pattern

The pattern that faster retests are associated with higher Continuation rates is the most robust finding
in this paper. It appears across all market regimes studied, survives logistic regression with multiple
controls, is independent of breakout time, and is not subject to the mechanical effect of the MFE_pre
definition.

One possible interpretive hypothesis, drawing on the microstructure concepts of Dim, Eraker, and
Vilkov (2023), is that fast retests occur when participants have limit orders placed near the breakout
level, waiting for a second entry opportunity. If those orders are sufficient to absorb the pullback
pressure, price returns quickly to the level and resumes its direction. A late retest would occur in a
context where that potential had been exhausted. This hypothesis is plausible as an interpretive
framework, but was not verified directly in this paper. It remains a concrete direction for future
research that would require order flow data not available in this study.

10.2 On the MFE_pre Pattern

The pattern that larger pre-retest excursions are associated with lower Continuation rates is
statistically robust but methodologically more delicate than the temporal pattern. As documented with
precision in Section 3.6, defining Continuation as exceeding MFE_pre creates a mechanical
relationship: the bar to clear grows with MFE_pre. Any analysis using this definition will tend to
show this pattern to some degree, regardless of what the market does.

What we cannot determine with the current design is what fraction of the observed pattern reflects a
genuine market phenomenon and what fraction is a consequence of the definition. We report the
pattern because it is consistent and because the question of its origin is itself valuable. The way to
resolve it in future research would be to compare results using a Continuation definition based on a
fixed target rather than a variable one. If the pattern persists under that alternative definition, it would
constitute stronger evidence that it reflects a genuine market phenomenon.

10.3 On the Statistical Difference of the 2022 Subperiod

Subperiod P4 (2022) shows a Continuation rate of 60.6%, statistically different from all other periods
at the highest level of significance (p<0.001). We report this difference as a statistical observation
without proposing a causal explanation. The mechanism is unknown and remains an open question
for future research.

10.4 Relationship with the Existing Literature

The Level 1 associations are consistent with Lundström (2017)'s result on ORB profitability scaling
with volatility, extended to the level of the individual breakout. Level 2 is consistent with Tsai et al.
(2018) on the concentration of price discovery at the open. The time-to-retest pattern has no direct
precedent in the literature known to us, which represents the most novel contribution of this paper.
10.5 Limitations

Limitation 1 — A single instrument over a single period. The observed patterns are specific to QQQ
over 2017–2024. Generalization to other instruments, markets, or periods requires independent
replication. This paper provides no basis for assuming that the same patterns, with the same numerical
values, would appear in another instrument or period.

Limitation 2 — No transaction costs. The analysis is conducted on the underlying price. No result
should be interpreted as evidence of profitability in live trading. Bid-ask spreads, execution slippage,
and commission costs are not incorporated.

Limitation 3 — The effect of definitions on results. As documented in detail in Section 3.6, two of the
observed patterns may be influenced by the chosen definitions. Continuation defined as exceeding
MFE_pre mechanically tends to produce lower rates when MFE_pre is large. Failure defined as
crossing the opposite OR extreme mechanically tends to produce lower rates when the range is wide.
We cannot precisely quantify how much of the numerical patterns observed for those variables is
attributable to these effects.

Limitation 4 — Multiple testing. The analysis evaluated multiple variables, bins, OR durations, and
subperiods without correction for multiple comparisons. Some results may be statistically significant
by chance. The most reliable patterns are those that appear consistently across multiple specifications.

Limitation 5 — Selection bias. Only the first breakout of the day is analyzed, and only retests that
satisfy the three defined conditions. The analyzed sample does not represent all QQQ trading days
but a filtered subset. The documented patterns apply to that specific subset.

Limitation 6 — Heterogeneous sample sizes across subperiods. P3 (2021) contains only 88 retests
with a 15-minute OR. Estimates for that subperiod have wide confidence intervals and should be
treated as indicative.

Limitation 7 — Temporal independence not formally verified. Daily observations may exhibit serial
autocorrelation. Autocorrelation-robust standard errors were not applied, which may artificially
inflate the reported statistical significance.

Limitation 8 — Mechanisms are unverified hypotheses. All causal interpretations proposed in Section
10 are plausible hypotheses grounded in microstructure literature. They were not verified using order
flow data or any other method independent of the one-minute prices used in this paper.
11. CONCLUSION
This paper documents, in an exploratory and descriptive manner and under explicit operational
definitions, four statistical associations in the post-breakout sequence of the ORB in QQQ, using
intraday data from 2017 to 2024.

We make no claims of causality. We make no claims of exploitability. We make no claims that these
patterns will persist outside the sample or replicate in other instruments. We do claim that we
measured them with the greatest care available to us, that we documented the ways in which our own
definitions may have influenced the results, and that we present this work as a first step in a direction
we consider valuable for research.

The four documented associations are as follows.

First, opening range size shows a linear and monotonic association with the observed maximum post-
breakout excursion, with a 2.8-fold difference between the minimum and maximum quintiles
(Z=+7.109, p<0.001). This pattern holds without exception across all 24 range durations studied.

Second, under the retest conditions defined in this paper, 68.4% of breakouts generate a retest of the
broken level within 120 minutes. The probability of that retest occurring grows monotonically with
the price excursion prior to the retest and approaches 100% when that excursion exceeds 0.50%.

Third, the time elapsed between the breakout and the retest shows the strongest and most robust
association with the outcome classified as Continuation. Retests within 10 minutes are associated
with a rate of 69.0% [95% CI: 62.5%, 74.8%] versus 25.6% [20.3%, 31.6%] for retests after 60
minutes (χ²=170.67, p<0.001). Logistic regression confirms that this association is independent of
breakout time (OR=0.722, p<0.001; breakout_minute not significant in any model). The pattern holds
across all five market regimes studied.

Fourth, larger pre-retest excursions are associated with lower Continuation outcome rates (Z=−7.697,
p<0.001; OR=0.537 in the multivariate model). This pattern is statistically robust but must be
interpreted with particular caution, as the Continuation definition used may mechanically produce
part of this result regardless of market behavior.

We present this work as an invitation to continue. The most important questions we leave open are
the following. Do these patterns replicate in SPY, SPX, IWM, and other liquid instruments, or are
they specific to QQQ in this period? Does the MFE_pre pattern persist when Continuation is redefined
using a fixed rather than variable target? What explains the statistical difference of the 2022
subperiod? Do data available from 2025 onward — not included in this analysis — confirm or
contradict the documented associations?

We hope that other researchers find in this paper a useful starting point, not a conclusion.
